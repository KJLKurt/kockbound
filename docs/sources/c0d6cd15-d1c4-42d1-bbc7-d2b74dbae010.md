# Push Arena — Game Design & Technical Foundation

## 1. High-Level Vision

**Working title:** Push Arena

**Genre:** Multiplayer arena / party combat / survival

**Platform initially:** Web browser, desktop-first with mobile support considered from the beginning

**Match size:** 4–12 players

**Match length:** Approximately 2–4 minutes

**Visual style:** Stylized 3D characters and environments viewed from an elevated/isometric camera

**Gameplay simulation:** Primarily two-dimensional movement and collision on a flat gameplay plane

**Business model:** Free-to-play with cosmetic progression and optional currency purchases; no pay-to-win gameplay advantages

The game should be easy to understand within seconds:

> Enter an arena, move, dash, avoid hazards, knock opponents out, survive, earn rewards, customize your character, and play again.

The deeper systems—character progression, cosmetics, challenges, maps, events, rankings, collections, seasonal content—give players reasons to return.

---

# 2. Core Design Pillars

Every future feature should support at least one of these pillars.

## Pillar 1 — Easy to Start

A new player should be able to load the game and enter their first match with almost no friction.

Initial controls:

- Move
- Dash
- One special ability
- Optional emote

There should not be twelve abilities, equipment stats, complicated builds, or dozens of menus before the first match.

## Pillar 2 — Difficult to Master

Simple controls can still produce deep gameplay through:

- timing
- positioning
- momentum
- environmental hazards
- player prediction
- ability timing
- map knowledge
- team coordination in applicable modes

## Pillar 3 — Short Matches

The game needs a strong:

**Play → Reward → Customize → Play Again**

loop.

Players should rarely feel trapped in a long match.

Target:

**2–4 minutes per standard game.**

## Pillar 4 — Personality

Characters should be expressive.

Players should immediately recognize:

- themselves
- teammates
- opponents
- rare cosmetics
- victory animations
- emotes
- special effects

The cosmetic system should become one of the game's major long-term systems.

## Pillar 5 — Fair Competition

Money must never directly buy:

- more damage
- faster movement
- stronger knockback
- additional health
- shorter cooldowns

Purchases should primarily affect appearance and expression.

# 4. Art Style

I would avoid attempting photorealism.

A better style is:

**stylized, colorful, polished, readable, slightly exaggerated 3D.**

Character proportions could be:

- large head
- recognizable body silhouette
- slightly oversized hands/feet
- strong facial expression
- clear costume silhouette

Think more along the design philosophy of animated games than realistic human characters.

Benefits:

- cosmetics are easier to see
- animation can be exaggerated
- lower polygon counts still look intentional
- browser performance improves
- AI-generated concept artwork translates better into stylized 3D assets
- assets age better visually

# 14. Matchmaking

Matchmaking should initially prioritize:

1. reasonable latency
2. fast queue time
3. roughly comparable player ability

Do not make matchmaking extremely complicated before there are enough players.

A new game's biggest matchmaking problem is usually **not skill balance**.

It is having enough simultaneous players.

Therefore version 1 should support bot filling.

Example:

```text
Player enters queue.

Wait up to a short threshold.

Players found: 5
Desired: 8

Start game with:

5 humans
3 bots

```

Bots should become less common as player population grows.

# Open-source assets are actually a very good idea

But I wouldn't tell Astra:

> "Find free assets."

That's how you end up with a Frankenstein game containing ten unrelated art styles.

Tell it instead:

> **Use permissively licensed assets as raw production material, then modify/retexture/recombine them until they conform to Push Arena's visual identity.**

There are some unusually useful libraries for what you're building.

For example, **Quaternius** has CC0 modular characters, outfits, GLB models, and a universal humanoid animation library with 130+ animations. That is almost tailor-made for establishing your animation/rigging pipeline. citeturn656772search3turn656772search4

**Kenney** also publishes its game assets under CC0 and allows commercial use and modification. citeturn656772search8

**Poly Haven** is excellent for HDRIs, textures and environmental source material, and its assets are CC0 as well. citeturn656772search0

That's a huge opportunity.

Rather than asking Astra to invent **every polygon from zero**, you might have it take:

> Quaternius-compatible humanoid rig  
> + modified/rebuilt proportions  
> + custom Push Arena face/head  
> + custom materials  
> + custom outfits  
> + common animation skeleton  
> + Push Arena-specific animations/VFX

and suddenly you have a dramatically more mature asset pipeline.

That's exactly the type of leverage I would want.

# Music needs slightly different treatment

This is one area where I would **not** expect Astra itself to magically become Hans Zimmer.

But Astra can be your audio director.

For Push Arena, I wouldn't create one endless background song.

I'd design **adaptive music**.

For example:

```text
MATCH START

Layer A
playful rhythmic foundation

0:45 / arena begins changing

Layer B
adds percussion / tension

1:30 / few players remain

Layer C
stronger melody / intensity

SUDDEN DEATH

Layer D
fast, chaotic climax

WINNER

music cuts
↓
short victory sting
```

Even better, create the tracks so the layers share tempo and bar structure.

Astra can manage transitions dynamically.

That will make the game feel far more expensive than simply having:

```text
background-song.mp3
```

running continuously.

# And SFX should be layered

Your document already hints at this, but I'd make it much more explicit.

Don't have:

LOW BODY IMPACT

WHOOSH

with randomized:

```text
weak hit → lighter layer

```

And combine that with your visual effects.

That's when:

> **WHAM — opponent goes flying**

starts becoming funny.

# I would give Astra this additional instruction

This is the part I'd add after your existing document.

:::writing{variant="document" id="58314" title="Push Arena — Astra Production Directive"}
## PUSH ARENA — PRODUCTION QUALITY DIRECTIVE

Treat the Game Design & Technical Foundation as the authoritative product direction, but do not interpret it as a requirement to implement the entire mature product immediately.

Your responsibility is not merely to make Push Arena functional. Your responsibility is to make it feel like a polished, intentional commercial game.

### General autonomy

Act as the project's technical director, gameplay engineer, art director, technical artist, UX designer, sound designer, and QA lead.

Make reasonable creative and technical decisions without asking me about routine details.

Ask me only when a decision:

- materially changes the identity of the game;
- is difficult or expensive to reverse;
- introduces a meaningful legal, financial, security, or operational commitment; or
- genuinely requires a personal preference that cannot reasonably be inferred.

When several reasonable approaches exist, choose the one you believe produces the best game, document the decision, and continue.

Do not stop development merely because every minor design detail has not been specified.

## Quality standard

"Technically working" does not mean "finished."

Do not accept placeholder-quality graphics, generic AI-looking interfaces, primitive geometry, arbitrary colors, generic particles, weak animations, temporary sound effects, or visually inconsistent assets as production-quality output.

Temporary assets are acceptable during prototypes but must be clearly identified as temporary.

For every player-facing feature, evaluate:

1. Does it work?
2. Does it feel responsive?
3. Does it look intentional?
4. Does it visually belong in Push Arena?
5. Does it sound satisfying?
6. Is the hierarchy/readability immediately understandable?
7. Does it maintain performance targets?
8. Would this look credible in a commercial game trailer?

If the answer to one of these is no, iterate.

## Visual development workflow

Before producing large quantities of assets, establish a coherent Push Arena visual language.

Create and maintain an ART_DIRECTION.md covering at least:

- character proportions;
- character silhouettes;
- facial design;
- environment design;
- shape language;
- material language;
- lighting;
- color philosophy;
- UI design;
- typography;
- animation style;
- particle/VFX style;
- cosmetic design;
- map readability;
- examples of desirable visual qualities;
- visual approaches that should be avoided.

Use concept artwork and reference boards before committing major assets.

Do not randomly create assets one-by-one without a common visual system.

Push Arena should feel like one fictional universe created by one art team.

## Character pipeline

Establish one production-quality base character and skeleton before producing large numbers of cosmetics.

Prefer a reusable humanoid skeleton that supports:

- idle;
- movement;
- dash;
- hit reactions;
- stun;
- falling;
- elimination;
- victory;
- emotes;
- future cosmetic animations.

Cosmetics should reuse the common skeleton whenever practical.

External CC0 models, rigs, animations, textures, and reference assets may be used as production foundations when doing so improves quality or development speed.

However, modify and integrate them so the final game has its own coherent Push Arena identity rather than looking like a collection of asset packs.

## External assets and licensing

You are authorized to research and obtain appropriately licensed game-development assets.

Prefer CC0/public-domain assets when practical.

Potential sources may include libraries such as:

- Kenney;
- Quaternius;
- Poly Haven;
- OpenGameArt;
- other reputable asset libraries discovered during research.

Do not assume an asset is reusable merely because it is downloadable.

Verify the license of every externally sourced asset before integrating it.

Do not copy or extract proprietary assets from commercial games.

Commercial games may be studied for broad design principles, polish, readability, game feel, camera behavior, animation timing, sound-design techniques, and UX patterns, but their protected assets should not be reproduced.

Maintain an asset provenance record containing:

- asset name;
- source;
- creator;
- source URL;
- license;
- acquisition date;
- modifications made;
- source production file;
- optimized runtime file.

## Sound effects

Major gameplay events should use layered sound design rather than a single generic sample.

For example, a strong dash impact may combine:

- transient impact;
- low-frequency body;
- movement whoosh;
- character reaction;
- environmental response.

Create multiple variations where repetition would otherwise become obvious.

Use controlled randomization of pitch, gain, or sample selection where appropriate.

Impact intensity should audibly correspond to gameplay intensity.

The player should be able to feel the difference between:

- a minor collision;
- a strong hit;
- a dangerous knockback;
- a major elimination.

---

## Game feel

Treat movement, collision, dash, knockback, animation, sound, camera behavior, particles, and timing as one combined system.

A successful hit should potentially combine:

- responsive movement;
- impact animation;
- squash/stretch;
- hit-stop or carefully controlled timing;
- camera impulse;
- particle burst;
- character reaction;
- appropriate sound;
- visible knockback;
- environmental interaction.

Use these effects deliberately rather than excessively.

The target emotion is playful competitive chaos.

The most important interaction in the game is:

**One player hits another player and sends them flying.**

That interaction must become exceptionally satisfying before large progression or monetization systems are built.

Follow the staged development strategy in the design document.

A stranger should be able to play the game without explanation and quickly understand:

**Move. Dash. Knock people off. Don't fall.**

Within several matches, the player should want:
